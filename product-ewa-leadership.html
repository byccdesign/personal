About

Context

EastWest Ageas is a Philippine life insurer formed through a partnership between EastWest Bank and Ageas Group. This case focuses on how I led the design team through its digital transformation.

The leadership challenge

Keep today moving while designing what comes next

The transformation touched sales, customer service, policy operations, and the systems connecting them. Teams needed to improve legacy workflows while researching, testing, and designing new platforms.

The central leadership challenge was deciding:

  • Which improvements to prioritize in the current experience
  • Which new platforms to design and test first
  • How to keep the team aligned on a shared direction while working in parallel

My leadership mandate

I was responsible for creating the conditions for the design team to deliver strong work across a complex transformation:

Create autonomy through clarity

Set direction across two horizons

I aligned designers around a connected insurance experience and balanced improvements to business-critical legacy workflows with discovery for future platforms. I allocated work across platform and journey workstreams, clarifying ownership and dependencies so the team could focus capacity on priority needs while moving in parallel.

Delegate decisions, not just tasks

My role was not to make every design decision. I established the principles, priorities, and context that allowed designers to make sound decisions independently, while staying available to coach through complex trade-offs.

Coach through regular feedback

Critiques, working sessions, and mentorship gave designers a regular space to test their thinking, strengthen their craft, and take ownership of more complex work. Weekly design updates made progress, risks, and open decisions visible across the team and its partners.

Influence through shared context

Align teams without relying on reporting lines

Business, technology, operations, compliance, and customer experience brought different priorities to the transformation. I worked with Product Owners, engineers, QA, business analysts, sales, marketing, department heads, and senior leaders to bring those perspectives into the same conversation.

Make trade-offs visible

Workshops, design reviews, and weekly updates connected customer needs and business goals with operational realities and technical constraints. Rather than advocating for design in isolation, I facilitated decisions around the shared evidence, trade-offs, and delivery priorities. This gave stakeholders a clearer view of progress and the decisions needed next.

Build a design practice that scales

The starting point: uneven design maturity

Design was often brought in at execution, and the organization’s understanding of design’s value and process varied. Teams lacked shared principles and a design system; tools and handoffs differed; work allocation and career development needed more structure; and important knowledge was not easy to find.

I focused on addressing these gaps by creating a shared, customer-centered practice that connected research and strategy to delivery.

01 — Hiring and team development

I introduced structured portfolio reviews and interview criteria, then created a competency matrix to guide designer development. Team and individual KPIs connected design quality, collaboration, and delivery goals; mentorship and regular critiques supported growth through the work.

02 — A clear operating rhythm

Design sprints, working sessions, critiques, and weekly updates gave designers a repeatable way to frame work, share progress, surface risks, and resolve decisions with partners.

03 — Research as part of the process

Over the first year, I brought interviews, focus groups, usability testing, prototyping, and A/B testing into the team's regular practice, so customer evidence could inform decisions before delivery.

Tools Used

04 — Shared standards and tools

I established the foundation for a shared design system and a new visual direction. We moved the legacy component library from Adobe XD to Figma and paired it with reusable patterns, documentation, accessibility expectations, and clearer engineering handoffs. Fortnightly Design System 101 sessions helped Product, Engineering, and QA adopt the shared foundation.

05 — Document how design works

I began building a shared Product Design playbook in Confluence, documenting our processes, tools, and ways of working for employees across the organization. It also gave new designers a practical guide to onboarding and navigating the team.

A foundation the team could keep building on

Within my first six months, I aligned the design team with business partners around a customer-centered vision: make insurance more accessible and approachable for Filipinos. I established the design-system foundation and shared practices to support that direction. After I left, the team continued developing the design system and building on the design culture we had started.
