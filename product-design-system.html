About

Context

A shared foundation for products at scale

Freelancer.com serves a global marketplace (80+ million users) with complex workflows and a growing ecosystem of digital products.

As the platform evolved, teams were working with different UI patterns, inconsistent component behaviours, and fragmented design documentation. Similar interface problems were being solved repeatedly, creating unnecessary design and engineering effort.

Challenge

Problem

When products grow independently, inconsistency becomes a system-wide problem.

As Freelancer.com expanded its product ecosystem, its interface patterns evolved across different teams, platforms, and legacy experiences.

Without consistent standards, familiar interactions could behave differently depending on where users encountered them. Designers and engineers also had to repeatedly interpret existing patterns or recreate solutions.

How do we create a shared design foundation that improves consistency and accessibility while giving individual products the flexibility to evolve?

Findings

Key Insights

Fragmented source of truth

Designers had difficulty finding the current, reusable component across files and products.

Incomplete component guidance

States, behaviors, and edge cases were not consistently documented from design through production.

Uneven foundations

Tokens and visual patterns were applied inconsistently, so similar experiences drifted.

Late system considerations

Accessibility and localization needs were not reliably part of component decisions from the start.

The Audit

Understanding the current state

Understanding the existing system before improving it

We reviewed the design files and mapped Bits from its foundations through components, states, and patterns. Connecting the parts helped product, design, and engineering discuss what should be shared across products and where teams needed product-specific flexibility.

The audit also exposed missing states and documentation gaps, giving the team a clearer basis for organizing and evolving the system.

The goal wasn't to replace everything. It was to strengthen what already existed and make the system easier to maintain and extend.

Strategy and Prioritization

Our approach

Focusing on high impact improvements

With UI engineers, QA, front-end infrastructure leads, and product owners, we prioritized improvements by component usage, product requirements, and technical feasibility.

  1. We focused on high-impact patterns such as forms, data tables, and navigation by refining existing components, expanding variants and interaction states, and introducing components where gaps existed.
  2. We included accessibility, localization, and cross-browser compatibility in the evaluation so each improvement supported a more consistent and inclusive experience.

Foundations

Establish the building blocks

Establishing a consistent visual language

I helped organize and document the core visual properties that define how interfaces look and behave. This included typography, color, spacing, and responsive layout guidelines.

Path to better foundations

  • Enabling theming and colour modes
  • Clean up and standardize existing tokens
  • Apply brand refresh colours and styles
  • Ensure accessibility compliance and cross-browser compatibility
  • Clean up and minimize SVG icons

The System

Architecture

One shared system. Multiple product identities.

Bits organized tokens in layers, from primitive values to semantic roles used by components. Product themes could supply brand-specific values to those semantic tokens while keeping component structure, states, and interaction guidance consistent.

The important distinction was defining what should remain consistent and what could change.

For example:

A button should preserve its interaction behaviour, accessibility requirements, and structural logic, while its visual treatment can adapt to the product's theme.

Components kept a shared structure, states, interaction, and accessibility guidance. Product themes changed the visual expression through semantic token values.

Theming

Family of Products

Theme the brand expression, not the component contract

The key decision was to define what a theme could change. Product-level values could express a distinct visual identity; component anatomy, states, behavior, and accessibility guidance stayed shared.

That boundary made the system adaptable across the product family without relying on one-off component overrides.

Inclusive by default

Accessibility and internationalization

Build accessibility and localization into component guidance

A global marketplace needs patterns that remain understandable across languages, devices, and abilities. Bits guidance covered accessible labels, familiar icon use, contrast, focus behavior, and flexible layouts so teams could account for these needs during design—not only at final QA.

Learnings

Adoption is more important than completeness

A design system doesn't become valuable simply because it contains hundreds of components. It becomes valuable when teams can confidently use it to solve real product problems.

Documentation is important and part of the system

A design system doesn't become valuable simply because it contains hundreds of components. It becomes valuable when teams can confidently use it to solve real product problems.Design system users are designers and engineers. They need the same level of clarity, usability, and guidance that we expect from the products we build.

Accessibility scales through better defaults

When accessibility considerations are embedded into component guidelines and design foundations, teams are better equipped to make inclusive decisions from the beginning.

Consistency doesn't mean uniformity.

A scalable design system needs to define what should remain consistent and where products should have flexibility. The challenge is creating shared standards without limiting the evolution of individual experiences.

Collaboration is key

Design systems are most effective when they are built and maintained collaboratively. Teams need to feel ownership of the system and have a clear process for contributing improvements.

Design systems are never finished

Design systems are living products that evolve with the needs of the organization. It requires continuous collaboration, maintenance, feedback, and iteration. Its success depends on how well it evolves alongside the products and teams it supports.
